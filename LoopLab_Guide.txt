1. WHAT LOOPLAB IS, IN ONE PAGE
-------------------------------

LoopLab is a workbench for thinking clearly about problems that keep coming
back.

Some problems are simple: a pump breaks, you replace it, it is fixed. Others
are stubborn: you add people to a late project and it gets later; you push for
speed and quality drops; you clear a backlog and it returns three months
later. Stubborn problems are almost never caused by one bad decision or one
bad person. They are caused by the way things are connected: A pushes B, B
pushes C, and C, some weeks later, pushes A again. These circles of cause and
effect are called FEEDBACK LOOPS, and LoopLab is built to find them, measure
them and test ways to break or use them.

LoopLab walks you through six stages, always in the same order:

   1. FRAME     - Say what the problem is, as a pattern over time.
   2. MAP       - Draw what causes what (a "causal loop diagram").
   3. ANALYZE   - Let the app find every loop and spot familiar patterns.
   4. QUANTIFY  - Turn the drawing into numbers and equations.
   5. TEST      - Run it forward in time, compare options, stress-test.
   6. DECIDE    - Rank the fixes by how deep they reach; write the brief.

At the end you have a decision brief: a short report that starts with the
recommendation and then shows the evidence behind it, including the loops that
explain the problem, the fixes that were tested and how they compared.

There is also an optional AI assistant (the COPILOT, which is Claude) that can
interview you, critique your diagram, explain loops in plain language and
draft the report. It only ever proposes changes; nothing changes until you
click accept.

Everything runs on your own computer, in your browser. Your model is saved in
the browser automatically. Nothing is sent anywhere unless you switch on the
copilot, and even then the secret key stays on your machine.


2. THE ONE DEFINING ADVANTAGE
-----------------------------

If you remember one sentence about LoopLab, remember this:

   LoopLab lets you test a fix on the structure of the problem BEFORE you
   spend real money, real people and real months on it.

Most organisations test fixes the expensive way: they try them on the real
project and wait to see. When the fix fails, months have passed, the budget is
spent, and nobody can tell whether the fix was wrong or whether something else
changed at the same time.

LoopLab replaces that with a cheap, fast rehearsal. You describe how the
problem works (what drives what), the app turns it into a small working model,
and you can try "add two engineers", "review designs earlier" or "move the
deadline" in seconds, side by side, on the same chart. Because the model is
built from cause and effect rather than from a trend line, it can show you the
side effects that a spreadsheet hides: the fix that works for six weeks and
then makes things worse, or the cheap change to a delay that beats the
expensive change to headcount.

Everything else in the app (the loop finder, the archetypes, the leverage
ranking, the report, the copilot) exists to make that rehearsal faster, more
honest and easier to explain to other people.


3. WHO IT IS FOR, AND WHEN TO USE IT (AND WHEN NOT TO)
------------------------------------------------------

Good fits:
- Project and engineering managers facing recurring slippage, rework, backlogs
  or "90 % done" syndrome.
- Planners and project-controls staff who need to explain WHY a schedule keeps
  slipping, not just THAT it slips.
- Quality, operations and maintenance leads with queues that never seem to
  clear.
- Anyone preparing a recommendation who wants to show the reasoning, not just
  the conclusion.
- Teachers and students of systems thinking and system dynamics.

Use LoopLab when:
- The problem has come back after being "fixed" before.
- People disagree about the cause, and each camp blames something different.
- Obvious fixes have side effects that arrive later.
- Delays are involved (procurement lead times, discovery of errors, hiring,
  approval cycles).
- You must justify a choice between several options to someone else.

Do not use LoopLab when:
- The problem is a one-off with a clear cause (a broken part, a missing
  signature).
- You need a precise day-by-day schedule. Use your scheduling tool for that.
  LoopLab models aggregates ("1,000 tasks", "2,000 spools"), not individual
  activities.
- You need a precise cost estimate. LoopLab explains dynamics; it is not an
  estimating tool.
- Nobody has time to think. LoopLab rewards one or two focused hours; it does
  not replace them.

A good rule: if you can draw the problem as a circle ("this causes that, which
causes more of this"), LoopLab will help. If you can only draw it as a
straight line ("this broke"), it probably will not.


4. HOW IT DIFFERS FROM THE TOOLS YOU ALREADY USE
------------------------------------------------

Spreadsheet.
A spreadsheet is excellent at arithmetic and terrible at feedback. Each cell
is computed once, from left to right. Real projects loop: pressure lowers
quality, low quality creates rework, rework adds pressure. A spreadsheet
cannot easily show that circle unfolding week by week. LoopLab is built around
it.

Scheduling tool (Primavera P6, MS Project and similar).
A schedule says what should happen, in what order, by when. It assumes
durations and productivity are fixed inputs. LoopLab asks what happens to
those durations and that productivity when the project is under pressure, when
errors are found late, when the supplier is slow. The two tools complement
each other: the schedule is the plan; LoopLab is a flight simulator for the
forces that bend the plan.

Risk register.
A risk register lists separate risks, each with a probability and an impact.
It treats them as independent. LoopLab shows how they feed each other: a late
delivery (risk A) raises pressure, which raises errors (risk B), which raises
rework (risk C). Often the "vital few" risks are the ones sitting on many
loops at once, and LoopLab ranks exactly that.

Fishbone diagrams and "5 whys".
These look for a ROOT cause, a starting point. They work well for
straight-line problems. For stubborn problems, the honest answer is often
"there is no single root; there is a loop". LoopLab is the next step after a
fishbone when the bones start pointing back at each other.

Lessons-learned reports.
These describe what happened. LoopLab lets you replay it and ask "what if we
had done X instead?", which turns a story into a testable explanation.

5. SYSTEMS THINKING IN FIVE MINUTES
-----------------------------------

A SYSTEM is a set of things connected so that together they produce a pattern
of behaviour over time. A project, a factory, a supply chain, a team and a
household budget are all systems.

Three ideas carry most of systems thinking:

Idea 1: Structure produces behaviour.
The same people in a different structure behave differently. If every project
in your company ends up "90 % complete" for months, the cause is unlikely to
be the people on each project. It is far more likely to be something they all
share: how progress is measured, how errors are discovered, how pressure is
applied. Change the structure and the behaviour changes.

Idea 2: Cause and effect go round in circles.
In everyday thinking, causes come before effects in a line. In systems,
effects come back to influence their causes. Overtime raises output (good),
which tempts management to raise targets, which requires more overtime, which
tires people, which lowers output. The circle matters more than any single
link.

Idea 3: Effects are delayed and far away.
The consequence of a decision often appears weeks or months later, and in a
different department. By the time it appears, nobody connects it to the
decision. A design shortcut taken in engineering shows up as rework on site
half a year later. Delays are why smart people repeat the same mistakes.

The practical habit that follows:
Before asking "who is to blame?" or "what is the fix?", ask "what is the
pattern over time, and what structure would produce exactly that pattern?"
LoopLab's six stages are this habit, turned into screens.


6. VARIABLES: THE THINGS THAT GO UP AND DOWN
--------------------------------------------

A VARIABLE is anything that can increase or decrease over time. Examples:
"Work to do", "Schedule pressure", "Quality", "Spools on site", "Open NCRs",
"Staff".

Good variable names are nouns or noun phrases that can be "more" or "less":
- Good: "Schedule pressure" (can be higher or lower).
- Bad: "Pressure increases" (that is an event, not a variable).
- Good: "Quality of work".
- Bad: "Bad quality" (that already contains a direction).
- Good: "Engineers on the project".
- Bad: "Hire engineers" (that is an action; the variable is the number of
  engineers).

A useful test: put "the level of ..." or "the amount of ..." in front of it.
If the sentence still makes sense, it is a variable.

Soft variables are allowed. "Morale", "Trust with the client" and "Perceived
progress" are hard to measure but very real, and leaving them out because they
are hard to measure is a common way to build a wrong model. LoopLab lets you
draw them first and attach numbers later (or never, if you only need the
qualitative picture).


7. CAUSAL LINKS AND THEIR SIGNS (+, -, ?)
-----------------------------------------

A CAUSAL LINK is an arrow from one variable to another, meaning "a change in
this causes a change in that".

Each link has a sign, called its POLARITY:

  + (same direction)
    If the cause goes up, the effect goes up (compared with what it would
    otherwise have been). If the cause goes down, the effect goes down.
    Example: Work to do -> Schedule pressure is "+". More work left, more
    pressure.

  - (opposite direction)
    If the cause goes up, the effect goes down. If the cause goes down, the
    effect goes up.
    Example: Schedule pressure -> Quality is "-". More pressure, lower
    quality.

  ? (unknown)
    You believe there is a link but you do not know which way it goes, or it
    depends. LoopLab lets you mark this honestly. Any loop through a "?" link
    is marked "U" (unknown type) until you decide.

Two important details:

"All else being equal."
A "+" link does not mean the effect always rises. It means that this cause, on
its own, pushes the effect up. Other causes may push it down at the same time.

Links can carry extra notes in LoopLab:
- A DELAY MARK (two short lines across the arrow in classic diagrams): the
  effect takes noticeably long to show.
- A MECHANISM NOTE: WHY the link exists, in one sentence. ("Under pressure,
  engineers skip self-checks.") This is the single most useful habit in causal
  mapping; it lets other people challenge the link instead of the conclusion.
- A CONFIDENCE level: low, medium or high.


8. FEEDBACK LOOPS: REINFORCING (R) AND BALANCING (B)
----------------------------------------------------

When a chain of links comes back to where it started, you have a FEEDBACK
LOOP. There are only two kinds.

REINFORCING LOOP (R) - the snowball.
A change goes round the loop and comes back bigger, in the same direction.
Reinforcing loops create growth or collapse that accelerates.
- Good example: a good reputation brings more clients, more clients bring more
  experience, more experience improves the reputation.
- Bad example (a vicious circle): rework raises pressure, pressure lowers
  quality, lower quality creates more rework.

BALANCING LOOP (B) - the thermostat.
A change goes round the loop and comes back as a correction, in the opposite
direction. Balancing loops pull things toward a goal or a limit.
- Example: the more work is left, the higher the pressure; the higher the
  pressure, the harder people work; the harder they work, the less work is
  left.
- Example: the fuller a warehouse, the fewer orders are placed, so it fills
  more slowly.

How to tell which is which (the counting rule):
Count the "-" links around the loop.
- An EVEN number of "-" links (0, 2, 4 ...) means REINFORCING (R).
- An ODD number of "-" links (1, 3, 5 ...) means BALANCING (B).
LoopLab does this counting for you, for every loop, automatically.

Why it matters:
Every pattern you see in a project is produced by loops taking turns to be in
charge. Early in a project a reinforcing loop may dominate (momentum builds);
later a balancing loop takes over (the remaining work runs out, or a limit is
hit). Knowing which loop is in charge, and when that changes, tells you where
to act. LoopLab measures this (see "Loop dominance" in chapters 22 and 39).


9. DELAYS: WHERE THE SURPRISES COME FROM
----------------------------------------

A DELAY is a gap in time between a cause and its effect.

Everyday delays:
- The shower takes a few seconds to respond when you turn the tap. People who
  do not wait turn it too far, get scalded, turn it back too far, and freeze.
  That swinging back and forth is called OSCILLATION, and it is caused by
  acting faster than the delay allows.
- A procurement lead time: spools ordered today arrive in four months.
- Discovery of errors: a design error made today may only be found at the next
  review, or on site.
- Hiring and training: a new engineer is not fully productive for months.
- Perception: a manager sees last month's report, not today's reality.

What delays do:
- They make balancing loops overshoot and oscillate (the shower).
- They hide the side effects of a decision until it is too late to connect
  them.
- They make good fixes look bad at first (and bad fixes look good at first).

Two kinds of delay, which LoopLab treats differently:
- MATERIAL DELAYS move real things through a pipeline: spools in transit, work
  in review. Nothing is lost; it just arrives later.
- INFORMATION DELAYS are slow perceptions: a smoothed average, a monthly
  report. The "thing" is a belief, which gradually catches up with reality.

A key lesson: shortening a delay is often far more powerful than adding
resources. Finding errors in four weeks instead of eight can beat hiring more
staff, because it changes WHEN the system learns, not just how much it can do.


10. STOCKS AND FLOWS: THE BATHTUB
---------------------------------

Picture a bathtub. The water in the tub is a STOCK: an amount that has built
up over time. The tap is an INFLOW and the drain is an OUTFLOW. Together they
are FLOWS: rates that change the stock.

Three facts about bathtubs explain a lot of project behaviour:

1. A stock changes only through its flows.
   You cannot change the amount of water directly; you can only change the tap
   or the drain. Similarly, you cannot change "work done" directly; only the
   rate of completing work changes it.

2. A stock keeps rising as long as the inflow is bigger than the outflow, even
   if the inflow is falling.
   This is why a backlog keeps growing for a while after a peak has passed:
   the peak has passed, the inflow is falling, but it is still above the rate
   at which work is cleared.

3. Stocks create delays and buffers.
   A stock smooths out differences between inflow and outflow. That is useful
   (a buffer of materials protects construction from a late delivery) and
   dangerous (a hidden stock of undiscovered errors protects the reported
   schedule, until it is discovered).

Project stocks: work to do, work done, undiscovered rework, staff, spools on
site, open NCRs, cash.
Project flows: work completed per week, errors made per week, errors
discovered per week, spools delivered per week, NCRs closed per week.

A quick test to tell them apart: if you could freeze time and take a
photograph, the things you could count in the photograph are stocks ("there
are 340 open NCRs"). The things that need a stopwatch are flows ("we close 10
NCRs per week").

In LoopLab, the causal loop diagram (arrows and signs) and the stock-and-flow
diagram (tubs, taps and arrows) are not two separate models. They are two
views of ONE model. That is why LoopLab calls them two "lenses": change the
model in one view and the other updates automatically, so they can never
disagree.


11. BEHAVIOUR OVER TIME: THE SHAPES TO RECOGNISE
------------------------------------------------

Before building a model, sketch the problem as a line on a chart: time across
the bottom, the thing you care about up the side. This sketch is called a
REFERENCE MODE. It is the behaviour you want the model to explain.

A handful of shapes appear again and again. Each one points to a structure:

Exponential growth (curving upward, faster and faster).
   Structure: a reinforcing loop in charge.
   Example: a backlog of rework that feeds itself.

Goal seeking (rising or falling quickly at first, then levelling off at a
target).
   Structure: a balancing loop in charge.
   Example: a team working down a fixed punch list.

S-shaped growth (slow start, fast middle, slow finish).
   Structure: a reinforcing loop that hands over to a balancing loop as it
   reaches a limit.
   Example: the classic project S-curve of cumulative progress.

Oscillation (going up and down around a target).
   Structure: a balancing loop with a significant delay.
   Example: hiring and firing in waves; stock levels swinging.

Overshoot and collapse (growing past a limit, then falling).
   Structure: growth that erodes the very resource it depends on.
   Example: overtime that burns out the team.

"Better before worse" (an improvement that later turns into a deterioration).
   Structure: a quick fix with a delayed side effect (see "Fixes that Fail").
   Example: skipping checks to meet a milestone, then paying for it in rework.

"The last 10 % takes forever" (reported progress racing ahead, then crawling).
   Structure: hidden rework discovered late.
   Example: the EPC rework example in Part 4.

In LoopLab you can sketch a reference mode by hand on a sketch pad, label it
historical, expected, feared or hoped, and see it drawn behind your simulation
results. When the model's line matches the shape of your sketch, you have a
structure that can explain the problem. When it does not, the model is missing
something, which is just as useful to know.


12. ARCHETYPES: THE STORIES THAT REPEAT
---------------------------------------

Over decades, systems thinkers noticed that a small number of loop structures
cause a large share of real-world problems. These recurring structures are
called SYSTEM ARCHETYPES. Each has a name, a typical story, a typical shape
over time and known ways to respond.

LoopLab includes eight:
- Fixes that Fail
- Shifting the Burden
- Limits to Growth
- Eroding Goals
- Escalation
- Success to the Successful
- Tragedy of the Commons
- Growth and Underinvestment

When you analyse your diagram, LoopLab compares its loops with these eight and
suggests the ones that might fit. A suggestion is only a hint. You confirm it
if the story matches your experience, or reject it if it does not. A confirmed
archetype brings its intervention advice into the Decide stage.

Why archetypes help:
They give a name to a pattern that people already feel but cannot describe.
Saying "this is Shifting the Burden: we keep using overtime instead of fixing
the staffing plan" is much more persuasive in a meeting than a long
explanation. All eight are described in chapter 37, each with a project
example.


13. LEVERAGE POINTS: WHERE PUSHING WORKS
----------------------------------------

Not all fixes are equal. The systems scientist Donella Meadows listed twelve
places to intervene in a system, from weakest to strongest. LoopLab uses her
list to tag every intervention you consider.

Shortened version (12 = weakest, 1 = strongest):
  12  Numbers and parameters (headcount, budgets, deadlines, targets)
  11  Sizes of buffers (stock levels, float, contingency)
  10  Physical structure (layout, routes, the way material moves)
   9  Lengths of delays (lead times, review cycles, reporting cycles)
   8  Strength of balancing loops (how strongly corrections respond)
   7  Strength of reinforcing loops (how fast vicious or virtuous circles
   turn)
   6  Information flows (who sees what, and when)
   5  Rules (incentives, penalties, procedures, contracts)
   4  The power to change structure (who can redesign the system)
   3  Goals (what the system is actually trying to achieve)
   2  Mindset or paradigm (the shared beliefs everything rests on)
   1  The ability to step outside any one paradigm

The uncomfortable truth:
Most proposed fixes on projects sit at level 12: more people, a new date, a
higher quota. They are easy to approve and easy to test, and they are often
the least effective, because the loops that cause the problem are left
untouched. Before recommending a level-12 fix, check whether a shorter delay
(9), better information (6) or a different rule (5) acts on the same loop more
durably. LoopLab puts the level next to every option so this question is
always visible. Each level is explained with a project example in chapter 38.


14. SIMULATION, IN PLAIN WORDS
------------------------------

To SIMULATE means to let the model run forward in time, step by step, and
record what happens.

How it works, without maths:
1. The app starts at time zero with each stock at its starting value.
2. It works out every flow from the current situation ("with this much
   pressure, quality is this, so this many flawed tasks are made per week").
3. It moves time forward one small step and updates each stock by its flows
   ("one quarter-week passed, so this many tasks moved from 'to do' to
   'done'").
4. It repeats until the end of the time horizon.

The step size is called DT ("delta time"). LoopLab's default is a quarter of a
time unit (for example a quarter of a week). Smaller steps are more accurate
and slower; for models of this size, both are fast (a large model with 500
variables runs 10,000 steps in under a third of a second).

There are two ways of taking each step:
- EULER (the default): simple and robust. Good for almost everything,
  especially models with sudden switches (a step change on a given date, a
  pulse).
- RK4 (Runge-Kutta 4): looks ahead within each step, so it is more accurate
  for smooth models. It loses that advantage when the model has sudden
  switches, and the app warns you when that combination occurs.

LoopLab checks itself: the Model Health panel re-runs your model with half the
step size and warns you if any stock moves by more than 1 %, which tells you
to choose a smaller DT.

What a simulation is NOT:
It is not a forecast. A model is a simplified, explicit statement of how you
believe the problem works. Its value is in comparing options ("A finishes
earlier than B, and here is why") and in exposing wrong beliefs ("if our
explanation were right, the backlog would have peaked in March; it peaked in
June, so something is missing"). Treat the absolute numbers with caution and
the comparisons with interest.

16. THE SCREEN LAYOUT
---------------------

The screen has four areas:

TOP BAR (across the top)
- The model's name.
- The save status (for example "Saved").
- Undo and redo buttons.
- A light/dark theme switch.
- The file menu (new, open, save, export) and the examples button.

LEFT RAIL (down the left side)
- The six stages: Frame, Map, Analyze, Quantify, Test, Decide.
- Click any stage at any time. The order is a guide, not a gate: you can jump
  back to Map from Test to fix a link, then return.
- Each stage shows only its own tools, so the screen never shows everything at
  once.

MAIN AREA (the middle)
- The canvas (the diagram) or the work area of the current stage: forms in
  Frame, the diagram in Map and Quantify, lists in Analyze, charts in Test,
  the comparison and report in Decide.

RIGHT DOCK (down the right side)
- Two tabs:
   - INSPECTOR: the details of whatever you have selected (a variable, a link,
     a loop). This is where you rename things, set a sign, write a mechanism
     note, type an equation or set a range.
   - COPILOT: the Claude panel (local version with a key only).
- The dock can be collapsed to give the canvas more room.

General behaviour:
- Common actions never open a pop-up window that blocks the screen. You rename
  things in place, edit in the Inspector, and messages appear briefly in a
  corner ("toasts") and fade away.
- Every change is saved automatically in the browser shortly after you make
  it.
- Undo goes back up to 200 steps.


17. STAGE 1 - FRAME
-------------------

Purpose: say clearly what you are trying to understand, before drawing
anything.

What is on the screen, and what to type:

Problem statement.
   One or two sentences describing a BEHAVIOUR OVER TIME, not a missing
   solution.
   - Good: "Reported progress reaches 90 % close to the deadline and then
     crawls, while the real finish slips by months."
   - Bad: "We need more engineers." (That is a solution in disguise.)

Purpose.
   What the model is for: "Explain the late finish and compare three
   responses."

Time horizon.
   Start, stop and time unit: for example 0 to 120 weeks. Make it long enough
   for delayed effects to show. A rule of thumb: several times the longest
   delay in the problem. If errors take 8 weeks to discover, 20 weeks is far
   too short.
   Advanced options (collapsed): the step size DT and the method (Euler or
   RK4). Leave the defaults unless Model Health tells you otherwise.

KPIs (key performance indicators).
   The few variables you will judge success by. For each, choose:
   - minimize (less is better, e.g. "Undiscovered rework"),
   - maximize (more is better), or
   - target, with a value (e.g. "True progress" with target 1, meaning 100 %).
   Keep it to two to four. The Test and Decide stages compare scenarios on
   these.

Reference modes.
   The pattern over time you want to explain (see chapter 11). Two ways to add
   one:
   - Sketch it with the mouse on the sketch pad, give it a name, and label it
     historical, expected, feared or hoped.
   - Import it from a CSV file with a time column and a value column (for real
     data).
   Reference modes are drawn faintly behind simulation results in Test, so you
   can see at a glance whether the model reproduces the pattern.

Boundary chart.
   Three lists that say what is inside and outside the model:
   - Endogenous (filled in by the app): variables that sit on feedback loops,
     i.e. explained by the model itself.
   - Exogenous (filled in by the app): inputs that come from outside and are
     not affected by the model.
   - Excluded (filled in by you): things you deliberately left out, each with
     a one-line reason.
   The excluded list is one of the cheapest ways to catch a wrong model. If a
   colleague reads it and says "but client changes are the whole problem!",
   you have learned something important in thirty seconds.

When Frame is done:
You can state the problem in one sentence, point at a sketch of its shape,
name the two to four KPIs, and say what you left out.


18. STAGE 2 - MAP
-----------------

Purpose: draw the CAUSAL LOOP DIAGRAM (CLD) - what causes what.

How to draw:
1. Press A (or use the add button) to add a variable. Type its name. Remember:
   a noun that can go up or down.
2. Press L to enter link mode. Click the cause, then the effect. An arrow
   appears, with "+" by default.
3. Select the arrow and press P to flip its sign between + and -. (Use the
   Inspector to set "?".)
4. Press D to toggle a delay mark on the selected arrow when the effect is
   slow.
5. In the Inspector, write a one-sentence mechanism note for each important
   link, and set your confidence (low, medium or high).
6. Press Shift+L to auto-arrange the diagram neatly. Anything you drag by hand
   stays where you put it.
7. Press F to fit the whole diagram on the screen.

All keyboard shortcuts:
   A               add a variable
   L               link mode
   P               flip the sign of the selected link
   D               toggle the delay mark
   Del/Backspace   delete what is selected
   Ctrl+Z          undo (Cmd+Z on a Mac)
   Ctrl+Y          redo (Shift+Cmd+Z on a Mac)
   Shift+L         auto-layout
   F               fit to screen
   ?               show the shortcut sheet

Naming rules the app enforces:
- Names are not case-sensitive ("Work Rate" and "work rate" are the same).
- Spaces and underscores count as the same character ("Work to do" =
  "Work_to_do").
- Built-in function names such as TIME or MIN cannot be used as variable
  names.

Tips:
- Close every loop you believe exists. A chain that does not come back to its
  start explains nothing dynamic.
- Start small: five to eight variables that capture the core story. You can
  always add more.
- Read each loop aloud as a story: "More pressure means lower quality, which
  means more flawed work, which later means more rework, which means more work
  to do and more pressure." If the story sounds wrong, a link is wrong.
- Mark delays generously. They are where most surprises come from.


19. STAGE 3 - ANALYZE
---------------------

Purpose: let the app study the structure you drew. You do not have to do
anything except read and decide.

What appears:

Loop list.
- Every feedback loop in the diagram, found automatically, with its type: R
  (reinforcing), B (balancing) or U (unknown, because it passes through a "?"
  link).
- Click a loop to highlight it on the diagram. Give it a name ("Rework cycle",
  "Haste makes waste").
- Self-loops (a variable that affects itself directly, like a tank draining
  itself) are included.
- Safety cap: in a very large, tangled diagram the number of loops can
  explode. The search stops at 1,000 loops by default and shows a warning, so
  the app never freezes. (The cap can be changed in the model settings.)

Loop participation and betweenness (for each variable).
- Participation: how many loops pass through it.
- Betweenness: how often it lies on the shortest paths between other
  variables.
- Variables high on both are the HUBS of your structure: the places where many
  loops meet. They are usually where interventions have the most effect, and
  where measurement matters most.

Archetype candidates.
- The app compares your loops with the eight archetypes and lists possible
  matches, each with the loops that match and a role mapping (for example:
  "problem symptom" = Schedule pressure; "fix" = Work harder).
- Click Confirm if the story fits your experience, or Reject if it does not.
  Only you can judge the story.
- A confirmed archetype carries its advice (with Meadows levels) into Decide.

Structural leverage map.
- Variables ranked by a structural score that combines loop participation,
  betweenness and whether they are stocks or delays.
- A Pareto line shows how few variables carry most of the structure. Usually
  two or three stand out.

Polarity consistency (once equations exist).
- When a variable has an equation, the app works out the sign the equation
  implies and compares it with the sign you drew. A mismatch is flagged. For
  example, if you drew "Quality -> Flawed work" as "+" but the equation is
  Work_rate * (1 - Quality), the app tells you the drawn sign is wrong.

When Analyze is done:
You know how many loops there are, which ones you believe matter, which
variables are hubs, and whether a known archetype fits.


20. STAGE 4 - QUANTIFY
----------------------

Purpose: turn the drawing into a working model by giving each variable a KIND
and an EQUATION. It stays the same model, now viewed through the
stock-and-flow lens.

The five kinds:

   KIND       MEANING                               ITS EQUATION HOLDS
   Stock      an accumulation (backlog, work done)  its starting value
   Flow       a rate into or out of stocks          the rate
   Auxiliary  an intermediate calculation           an expression
   Constant   a fixed number                        a number, plus an
                                                    optional min-max range
   Lookup     a graphical function (a table/curve)  a table, edited in the
                                                    lookup editor

Notes:
- A variable you have not yet quantified keeps the kind "variable". It is
  shown dashed and blocks simulation until you give it a kind and an equation.
- Draw a flow between a stock and another stock, or between a stock and a
  "cloud" (the edge of the model, where things come from or go to). Connecting
  a flow to a stock automatically creates the matching causal link: "+" into
  the stock it fills, "-" into the stock it drains.
- Give every uncertain constant a RANGE (lowest and highest believable value).
  The sensitivity tool in Test uses these ranges.
- Lookups describe effects that are not straight lines, for example "pressure
  raises productivity a little at first, then not at all". You draw the curve
  as a small table.

The equation language (quick reference):

Names.
   Write spaces as underscores, e.g. Work_to_do / Time_remaining, or put the
   name in double quotes: "Work to do". Not case-sensitive. The editor
   autocompletes names and functions as you type.

Arithmetic and logic (strongest first):
   ^                         power
   -x, NOT                   negative, logical not
   *  /  MOD                 multiply, divide, remainder
   +  -                      add, subtract
   <  <=  >  >=              comparisons
   =  <>                     equal, not equal
   AND, then OR
   IF condition THEN a ELSE b

Test inputs (to create a change on purpose):
   STEP(height, time)                a jump of "height" at "time"
   PULSE(volume, first[, interval])  a one-off (or repeating) burst
   RAMP(slope, start[, end])         a steady climb from "start"

Delays and smoothing:
   SMTH1 (also SMOOTH), SMTH3 (also SMOOTH3), SMTHN
      Information delays: slow perceptions that catch up gradually.
   DELAY1, DELAY3, DELAYN
      Material delays: things in a pipeline; nothing is lost.
   DELAY(input, time)
      A fixed pipeline delay: exactly "time" later.
   PREVIOUS, INIT
      The value one step ago; the value at the start.

Maths:
   MIN(a, b), MAX(a, b), ABS, EXP, LN, LOG10, SQRT, INT,
   SIN, COS, TAN and their inverses, PI,
   SAFEDIV(a, b[, x])   divide, but give x (default 0) instead of
                        an error when b is zero

Time:
   TIME (the current time), DT, STARTTIME, STOPTIME

There are no random-number functions, on purpose. Every run of the same model
gives exactly the same result, which makes comparisons trustworthy.
Uncertainty is explored in the Test stage instead.

Units.
- Give every variable a unit: tasks, tasks/week, people, tasks/(people*week),
  1/month, meter^2, or dmnl (dimensionless, for fractions and ratios).
- Time units: second, minute, hour, day, week, month, quarter, year.
- Declare your own base units (tasks, spools, people, USD ...) in the model's
  unit list.
- The golden rule: a stock's unit must equal its flows' unit multiplied by
  time. "Tasks" is filled by "tasks/week". The app checks this.


21. MODEL HEALTH: THE BUILT-IN CHECKER
--------------------------------------

The Model Health panel watches the model continuously and points at the
elements involved, with a "Show" button that jumps to them. It checks:

- Unit consistency. You cannot add tasks to people, compare weeks with tasks,
  or fill a stock of tasks with a flow of people.
- Undefined or unused variables. An equation names something that does not
  exist, or a variable is never used.
- Links that do not match equations. Every variable should have an arrow from
  exactly the variables its equation uses. If the equation uses Staff but
  there is no arrow from Staff, the diagram is lying, and the app says so.
- Algebraic loops. A circle of calculations with no stock or delay in it (A
  needs B, B needs A, at the same instant). The circle is named so you can
  break it.
- Integration error. The model is re-run with half the step size. If any stock
  moves by more than 1 % (adjustable), it warns you. The fix: lower DT to a
  quarter or an eighth of the shortest time constant in the model.
- Your own assertions. You can write rules that must always hold, such as
  Work_to_do >= 0. They are checked at every saved step and the first
  violation is reported.
- Polarity consistency. Drawn signs against the signs the equations imply (see
  chapter 19).
- Numeric problems. Division by zero, or values that become infinite or "not a
  number".

Severity labels tell you what to fix first. A clean Model Health panel does
not prove the model is right, but a model with health problems is certainly
not ready to be trusted.


22. STAGE 5 - TEST
------------------

Purpose: run the model, compare options and find out which uncertainties
matter.

The run bar (top of the Test screen):
- "Run" (shown as a play symbol). While it runs, the label shows "Running..."
  and a Cancel button appears.
- Next to it, a summary: the method (Euler or RK4), the DT, the time horizon,
  and how long the last run took.
- "Export CSV": saves the latest run's numbers as a spreadsheet file.
- You can change the method (Euler or Runge-Kutta 4), the DT and the horizon
  here.

Charts and runs:
- The main chart shows the selected variables over time. A side panel ("Series
  and runs") lets you choose which variables to show and lists your kept runs.
- "Runs (compare)": keep several runs and show them on the same chart. Each
  run can be shown, hidden or removed.
- Reference modes from Frame appear faintly behind the lines.
- Simulation runs in the background, so the screen stays responsive.

Scenarios:
- A scenario is a named set of changes to the base model: a different value
  for a constant, a different equation, and optionally different simulation
  settings.
- Examples: "Add two engineers" (Staff = 12 instead of 10), "Earlier design
  reviews" (Time to discover rework = 4 instead of 8).
- Run each scenario and keep it to compare against the base run.

One-at-a-time sensitivity (the tornado):
- For every constant that has a range, the app runs the model twice: once with
  the constant at its lowest value and once at its highest, everything else at
  its base value.
- It measures how much the KPI swings between the two runs.
- Result: a TORNADO chart, with the biggest swing at the top, plus a PARETO
  line showing the cumulative share. Typically two or three parameters account
  for most of the uncertainty. Those are the numbers worth measuring properly;
  the rest can stay rough.
- Current limitation: the tornado uses the first KPI, at the end of the run.

Loop dominance (Loops That Matter):
- For every moment in the run, the app calculates each loop's share of what is
  driving the change in the model.
- A loop is DOMINANT when its share reaches 50 %. If no single loop reaches 50
  %, the smallest group of loops that together reach 50 % is shown.
- The chart shows how control passes from one loop to another over time. For
  example, a growth loop in charge early on hands over to a limiting loop
  later. That handover moment is often the best time to act.
- This uses a published method ("Loops That Matter", 2023 revision).
- Current limitation: loops whose links pass through a smoothing or delay
  function (SMTH, DELAY) score zero, so they may be under-reported.

Not yet available in the app (see chapter 44): Monte Carlo (thousands of
random combinations of the ranges) and automatic calibration to real data.


23. STAGE 6 - DECIDE
--------------------

Purpose: turn what you learned into a choice, and write it down.

Interventions.
For each option you are considering, record:
- A name ("Hold design reviews earlier").
- Its Meadows leverage level (12 = weakest ... 1 = strongest; see chapter 38).
- The scenario that tests it. Choose an existing scenario from the list, or
  click "Add scenario" and give a name, one constant and its new value.
- A status: idea -> tested -> recommended (or rejected).
- A rationale: one or two sentences explaining why.

Side-by-side comparison.
A table of the KPIs for each tested scenario against the base run, so you can
see at a glance which option moves which KPI, and by how much.

Report export.
Produces the decision brief (chapter 24) as Markdown, which can be printed to
PDF from the browser.

A healthy habit in Decide:
For each level-12 option (more people, new dates), ask whether a level 9, 6 or
5 option acts on the same loop. Write down the answer in the rationale, even
if you still choose the level-12 option. That one sentence is often what
convinces a sceptical reader.


24. THE REPORT (DECISION BRIEF)
-------------------------------

The report is written in "pyramid order": the answer first, the evidence
after. Busy readers can stop after the first section.

Its sections, in order:
1. Recommendation: what to do, in a few lines.
2. The loops that explain the problem, named and described.
3. The leverage ranking: the options considered, with their Meadows levels.
4. The evidence: model health, sensitivity (tornado), loop dominance.
5. Simulation charts comparing the scenarios.
6. Appendix: the full model listing (every variable, equation and unit),
   assumptions, the boundary chart and sources.

Two safety features:
- Model text (names, notes) is treated strictly as data in the report, so
  nothing written in a model can run as code when the report is opened.
- The CSV export guards against "formula injection": a cell that starts with
  "=", "+", "-" or "@" cannot run as a formula when opened in a spreadsheet.

How to use it:
Send the recommendation section as the body of an email and attach the full
report. The appendix lets a technical reviewer check every assumption, which
is what makes a model-based recommendation credible.

==============================================================================
PART 4 - FULL WORKED EXAMPLE: THE "90 % DONE FOR MONTHS" PROJECT
==============================================================================

This part walks through the bundled example "EPC project rework cycle" from
start to finish, click by click. Open it now if you can (examples button in
the top bar -> "EPC project rework cycle") and follow along. Every name below
is the exact name you will see on screen.

A note on numbers: the results quoted here (week 53, week 76 and so on) come
from the example's documentation. If the screen shows slightly different
values, trust the screen; the shape of the story is what matters.


26. THE STORY
-------------

An EPC (engineering, procurement, construction) contractor has an engineering
package of 1,000 deliverables (drawings, datasheets, calculations), 10
engineers and a 52-week deadline.

What everyone sees:
- Progress reports look healthy for most of the year: 40 %, 60 %, 75 %, 85 %.
- Near the deadline, reported progress reaches about 90 %, and then it crawls.
  Week after week, it says 91 %, 92 %, 93 %.
- The real finish slips by months. The planned year becomes well over a year.
- Management responds with pressure: overtime, daily progress meetings, "just
  get it out the door".

What nobody sees:
Some deliverables marked "done" contain errors. Nobody knows yet. They are
found weeks later, in a review, by another discipline, or by the client, and
they come back as rework. Meanwhile the progress report counted them as
complete. And because the report looks better than reality, the pressure is
aimed at the wrong time: too little early, too much late, when haste creates
even more errors.

This pattern is so common in project management that it has a name: the "90 %
syndrome". The rework cycle that causes it is one of the best-documented
structures in the project-dynamics research (Lyneis and Ford, 2007).

Our questions:
1. Can a simple structure reproduce the pattern?
2. Of three responses (add engineers, review designs earlier, set a realistic
   deadline), which helps most, and why?


27. STEP 1 - FRAME IT
---------------------

Click "Frame" in the left rail. You will see what the example's author filled
in:

Problem statement:
   "Engineering packages report ~90 % complete for months while the true end
   date keeps slipping; the planned 52 weeks become well over a year."
   Notice: it describes a behaviour over time, not a solution.

Purpose:
   "Explain the overrun through the rework cycle and schedule pressure, and
   compare policies: more staff, earlier discovery of rework, honest progress
   reporting."

Time horizon:
   0 to 120 weeks, with DT = 0.25 week and Euler integration. Why 120 when the
   deadline is 52? Because the interesting part happens AFTER the deadline. A
   52-week horizon would cut off the very behaviour we want to see.

KPIs (three):
   - True progress: target 1 (that is, 100 % genuinely complete).
   - Undiscovered rework: minimize (hidden errors are the enemy).
   - Schedule pressure: target 1 (1 means "the normal pace is just enough";
     above 1 means the team must work faster than normal to make the date).

Reference mode:
   "Reported progress stalls near 90 %", labelled FEARED. It is a hand sketch
   through these points: 0 % at week 0, 40 % at week 20, 75 % at week 40, 88 %
   at week 52, 93 % at week 65, 97 % at week 80 and 100 % at week 95.

Excluded (the boundary chart), each with a reason:
   - Hiring, training and experience dilution. (Staff is treated as a simple
     lever; the effect of new people slowing down experienced ones is left out
     for simplicity.)
   - Client scope changes. (Scope is fixed, to isolate the rework dynamics.)
   - Fatigue from sustained overtime. (Folded into the effect of pressure on
     quality.)
   - Knock-on effects on procurement and construction. (Covered by the
     separate handoff example.)

Pause and read the excluded list. It tells you what this model CANNOT answer.
For example, it cannot tell you whether hiring would be slowed down by
training time, because training is outside the model. If that is your real
question, the model needs to be extended first.


28. STEP 2 - MAP IT
-------------------

Click "Map". You see the causal loop diagram. Three loops tell the story:

Loop 1: The rework cycle (R, reinforcing).
   Work rate -> Flawed work -> Undiscovered rework -> Rework discovery -> Work
   to do -> Work rate.
   In words: the more work is done, the more flawed work is produced; flawed
   work sits undiscovered; after a delay it is discovered and returns to the
   pile of work to do; which keeps the work rate going. Every flawed task
   comes back later.

Loop 2: Work harder (B, balancing).
   Work to do -> Schedule pressure -> Productivity -> Work rate -> less Work
   to do.
   In words: more work left means more pressure; pressure makes people work
   faster; faster work reduces the work left. This is the "good" correcting
   loop that management relies on.

Loop 3: Haste makes waste (R, reinforcing).
   Schedule pressure -> lower Quality -> more Flawed work -> more Undiscovered
   rework -> (later) more Work to do -> more Schedule pressure.
   In words: pressure lowers quality; low quality creates hidden errors; the
   errors return as rework; rework raises pressure. A vicious circle, with a
   delay inside it.

Click "Schedule pressure" and read its note in the Inspector:
   "Work rate needed to finish by the deadline relative to normal capacity.
   Only visible work counts: undiscovered rework is invisible."
This one sentence is the heart of the problem. The pressure signal is computed
from the work people can SEE. Hidden errors do not create pressure until they
are discovered, which is exactly when it is too late to absorb them calmly.

Try it: select the link "Quality -> Flawed work" and look at its sign. It is
"-" (lower quality means more flawed work), with the note "Lower quality ->
more flawed work." Press P to flip it to "+" and watch the loop types in
Analyze change, then press Ctrl+Z to undo. This shows how one sign can turn a
vicious circle into a harmless one, which is why each sign deserves a moment's
thought.


29. STEP 3 - ANALYZE IT
-----------------------

Click "Analyze".

Loop list:
   The app finds 10 loops: 4 reinforcing and 6 balancing. The rework cycle and
   haste-makes-waste loops are among the reinforcing ones. Click each loop to
   see it highlighted. Name the important ones ("Rework cycle", "Work harder",
   "Haste makes waste") so they appear by name in the report.

Hubs:
   "Work to do" lies on 9 of the 10 loops. "Work rate", "Schedule pressure"
   and "Flawed work" each lie on 6. These are the hubs: the places where many
   loops meet. Notice that "Schedule pressure" is a hub. That is a strong hint
   that how pressure is calculated (and what it can see) matters a great deal.

Archetype candidates:
   The matcher may suggest FIXES THAT FAIL: the "fix" is pressure-driven haste
   (work harder, faster), and the "unintended consequence" is the delayed
   rework that haste creates. Read the role mapping. If it matches your
   experience of projects, click Confirm. Its advice ("look for the delayed
   side effect; weaken it, or find a fix without one") will appear in Decide.

Structural leverage map:
   Look at the Pareto line. A handful of variables (the hubs above, plus the
   delay "Time to discover rework") carry most of the structure.

What we have learned without a single number:
The problem is not "engineers are slow". It is a loop of hidden errors and
misdirected pressure, and it runs through a delay (the time it takes to
discover rework) and a blind spot (pressure ignores undiscovered rework).


30. STEP 4 - QUANTIFY IT
------------------------

Click "Quantify". The same model now appears as stocks and flows.

Three stocks (the tubs):
   - Work to do (tasks). Starts at Project_scope (1,000). Cannot go below
     zero. Its note: "Tasks not yet attempted, plus discovered rework."
   - Work done (tasks). Starts at 0. "Tasks done correctly."
   - Undiscovered rework (tasks). Starts at 0. "Tasks believed done that
     contain errors nobody has found yet."

Three flows (the taps):
   - Correct work = Work_rate * Quality
        from Work to do into Work done.
   - Flawed work = Work_rate * (1 - Quality)
        from Work to do into Undiscovered rework.
   - Rework discovery = Undiscovered_rework / Time_to_discover_rework
        from Undiscovered rework back into Work to do.
        (On average, a hidden error is found after Time_to_discover_rework
        weeks.)

The auxiliaries (the calculations):
   - Work rate = MIN(Staff * Productivity, Work_to_do / Minimum_task_duration)
        The team's capacity, but it slows down as the pile runs out (you
        cannot work faster than the remaining work allows).
   - Time remaining = MAX(Deadline - TIME, Minimum_time_remaining)
        Weeks left until the deadline, but never less than 4. After the
        deadline passes, people plan on "about four more weeks", again and
        again.
   - Schedule pressure = Work_to_do / (Time_remaining * Staff *
     Normal_productivity)
        The pace needed, divided by the normal pace. Note: it uses Work_to_do
        only, not Undiscovered_rework.
   - Effect of pressure on productivity: a lookup (curve). At pressure 1 or
     below, no effect. At 1.5, +10 %; at 2, +20 %; at 3, +25 %. Overtime
     helps, but with diminishing returns.
   - Effect of pressure on quality: a lookup. At pressure 1 or below, no
     effect. At 1.5, quality is 90 % of normal; at 2, 80 %; at 3, 70 %.
   - Productivity = Normal_productivity * Effect_of_pressure_on_productivity
   - Quality = Normal_quality * Effect_of_pressure_on_quality ("Fraction of
     work done without errors.")
   - True progress = Work_done / Project_scope
   - Perceived progress = (Work_done + Undiscovered_rework) / Project_scope
        "What progress reports show: flawed work counts as done until
        discovered." This is the variable that produces the 90 % syndrome.

Constants, each with an uncertainty range [low, high]:
   Project scope ........... 1000 tasks          [900, 1100]
   Staff ................... 10 people           [8, 12]
   Normal productivity ..... 2 tasks/person/week [1.6, 2.4]
   Normal quality .......... 0.8 (80 % right)    [0.7, 0.9]
   Time to discover rework . 8 weeks             [4, 16]
   Deadline ................ 52 weeks            [44, 60]
   Minimum time remaining .. 4 weeks             [2, 8]
   Minimum task duration ... 1 week              [0.5, 2]

A quick sanity check you can do in your head:
   10 engineers x 2 tasks per week = 20 tasks per week. 1,000 tasks / 20 = 50
   weeks, just inside the 52-week deadline. But only 80 % of the work is right
   first time, so roughly 200 tasks will need doing again, and some of those
   will be flawed too. The plan had no room for rework at all. The model makes
   this visible.

Model Health:
   The panel should be clean: units consistent, every link matching its
   equation, no algebraic loops. Two assertions are written and hold:
   Work_to_do >= 0 ("Backlog cannot be negative.") and Undiscovered_rework >=
   0.


31. STEP 5 - TEST IT
--------------------

Click "Test" and press Run.

Base run - what you should see:
   - Perceived progress (what the reports show) climbs steadily and passes 90
     % around week 53, right at the deadline. Then it crawls.
   - True progress lags behind it all year. It reaches 99 % only around week
     76: an overrun of about six months.
   - The faint reference sketch ("feared") sits close to the
     perceived-progress line. The structure reproduces the problem.
   - Schedule pressure stays near 1 for months, then climbs steeply as the
     deadline approaches, because the visible pile is not shrinking fast
     enough.
   - Quality falls as pressure rises (haste makes waste).
   - Undiscovered rework builds up and peaks near the deadline: a hidden pile
     of "done" work that is not done.

Why the last 10 % takes so long:
   When reported progress is 90 %, some of that 90 % is undiscovered rework.
   As it is discovered, it moves back into Work to do, so the team keeps
   finishing work while the pile refuses to shrink. Meanwhile pressure is
   high, quality is low, and part of the rework is itself done wrong. The
   loops explain the crawl without anyone being lazy or incompetent.

Comparing scenarios:
   Run each scenario and keep the runs, so they appear together on one chart:
   - "Add two engineers" (Staff = 12 instead of 10): finishes around week 65.
   - "Earlier design reviews" (Time to discover rework = 4 weeks instead of 8,
     "rework found in about half the time"): finishes around week 66. The peak
     of undiscovered rework drops by about 40 %, because errors surface while
     there is still time to absorb them calmly.
   - "Plan for rework" (Deadline = 64 weeks instead of 52, "deadline set with
     expected rework included"): finishes only slightly earlier than the base
     run.

   The surprise is the third one. A realistic deadline removes the pressure
   but not the rework, so on its own it barely helps. The first two are close
   on finish date, but they work very differently: one buys capacity (it costs
   salaries every week), the other changes when the system learns about its
   own errors (it costs a change in review practice).

Sensitivity (the tornado):
   Run sensitivity. Each of the eight constants is pushed to the low and high
   ends of its range, one at a time. The tornado ranks them by how much they
   swing the first KPI (True progress, at the end of the run). The Pareto line
   shows how few parameters carry most of the uncertainty. Whatever comes out
   on top is the number most worth measuring properly on a real project. (On
   the original example, read the order from the screen.)

Loop dominance:
   Open "Loop dominance". Watch which loops are in charge over time: the
   work-harder loop early on, and the rework and haste loops taking a larger
   share as the deadline approaches. The moment the reinforcing loops take
   over is the moment the project becomes hard to steer, which tells you WHEN
   an intervention has to be in place: before that handover, not after.


32. STEP 6 - DECIDE
-------------------

Click "Decide". The example ships three interventions:

   INTERVENTION                         LEVEL  TESTED BY        RESULT

                                               engineers
   Hold design reviews earlier          9      Earlier design   about week 66,
                                               reviews          peak hidden
                                                                rework -40 %
   Report progress on verified          6      (needs a         not simulated
   work only                                   structural       in the bundled
                                               change)          model

Reading the table with leverage in mind:
- "Add two engineers" is level 12, a parameter. It works, but it costs money
  every week, and it does not touch the loops: hidden errors are still found
  late and pressure is still blind.
- "Hold design reviews earlier" is level 9, a delay. It gets almost the same
  finish date and also halves the delay before errors are found, which shrinks
  the hidden pile. It is usually much cheaper than two extra engineers for a
  year.
- "Report progress on verified work only" is level 6, an information flow. It
  fixes the blind spot itself: pressure would respond to the real situation,
  early, when it is cheap to respond. Testing it needs a structural change
  (let Schedule pressure see an estimate of Undiscovered rework). You can make
  that change yourself in Quantify, or, in the local version with a key, ask
  the copilot in Intervene mode to propose it as a patch and simulate it.

Writing the recommendation (an example):
   "Hold design reviews early and report progress on verified work only; add
   staff only if the finish date still misses. Early reviews achieve nearly
   the same finish as two extra engineers by halving the time hidden errors
   stay hidden. A deadline extension alone does not help, because it removes
   pressure but not rework."

Set the statuses (recommended, tested, idea), write a one-line rationale for
each, and export the report. It opens with the recommendation, then the loops,
the leverage ranking and the evidence.


33. WHAT THE EXAMPLE TEACHES
----------------------------

1. The problem was in the structure, not the people. Nobody in the model is
   lazy. The 90 % syndrome is produced by hidden rework, a discovery delay and
   pressure that cannot see the hidden pile.

2. The obvious fix (more people) works, but at the shallowest level and the
   highest running cost.

3. A cheaper change to a delay (earlier reviews) gives a similar result, and
   it reduces the hidden risk instead of outrunning it.

4. The "realistic" fix (a later deadline) barely helps on its own. This is a
   common and costly surprise in real projects.

5. The deepest fix (honest progress information) targets the blind spot
   directly. The model also tells you exactly what you would need to change to
   test it.

6. Without the model, these five points are opinions. With it, they are a
   reproducible argument that a colleague can check, challenge and re-run.

34. ENGINEERING -> PROCUREMENT -> CONSTRUCTION HANDOFF
------------------------------------------------------

The story:
   "Piping crews were mobilised on the planned date but sat partly idle for
   months waiting for spools, and mechanical completion still slipped."

What it models:
   2,000 piping spools pass through three stages:
   - Engineering issues isometric drawings (80 spools per week, starting in
     week 4, once basic design is frozen).
   - Procurement buys, fabricates and ships each spool. The average lead time
     is 16 weeks, modelled as a third-order material delay (DELAY3), so
     deliveries spread out realistically instead of arriving all at once.
   - Construction installs delivered spools (crew capacity 60 spools per
     week), with crews mobilised in week 12.

   The catch: the mobilisation date was "planned on an assumed 8-week
   procurement lead time". The real lead time is 16 weeks.

Stocks: Spools to engineer, Spools in procurement, Spools on site, Spools
installed.
Flows: Isometric issue, Spool delivery (the DELAY3), Installation.
Key auxiliary: Idle crew capacity = Construction capacity - Installation:
"installation capacity paid for but not used because spools are missing".

KPIs: Installed fraction (target 1) and Idle crew capacity (minimize).
Reference mode: "Installation S-curve lags the plan" (historical).
Excluded: engineering rework and design changes (see the rework example);
area-by-area prioritisation (one aggregate commodity keeps it simple); other
commodities such as steel, equipment, E&I (same structure).

Simulation: 0 to 80 weeks, DT = 1/16 week.

Scenarios and interventions:
   - "Mobilise crews later" (mobilisation at week 22 instead of 12): "Mobilise
     when spools are actually on site." Intervention level 12: "Changes a date
     (a parameter) to cut idle capacity."
   - "Shorter procurement lead time" (10 weeks instead of 16): "Frame
     agreements with the spool fabricator." Intervention level 9: "Shortens
     the longest delay in the chain."
   - "Start isometrics earlier" (week 2 instead of week 4).

What to look for when you run it:
   - In the base run, crews arrive in week 12 but spools trickle in, so Idle
     crew capacity is high for weeks: you are paying for crews with nothing to
     install.
   - Mobilising later cuts the idle cost but does not move the installation
     curve much: the spools still arrive when they arrive.
   - Shortening the lead time moves the whole installation S-curve to the
     left. It attacks the delay that actually governs the finish.
   - Starting engineering two weeks earlier helps a little, because the lead
     time, not engineering, is the bottleneck.

The lesson:
   In a chain of stages, the longest delay usually sets the pace. Adjusting
   dates around a delay (level 12) saves cost; shortening the delay itself
   (level 9) saves time.


35. QC INSPECTION AND NCR BACKLOG
---------------------------------

The story:
   "During the construction peak the QC inspection backlog exploded; months
   after the peak, crews still wait weeks for hold-point releases."

What it models:
   Inspection requests (for example welds and test packs) wait for QC.
   Inspected items pass or are rejected. Rejected items become NCRs
   (non-conformance reports), are repaired, and come BACK to the inspection
   queue to be re-inspected.

Stocks: Awaiting inspection, Open NCRs, Accepted.
Flows: Submissions, Items passed, Items rejected, NCRs closed (repaired items
return for re-inspection).
Key auxiliary: Inspection waiting time = Awaiting_inspection /
Inspection_capacity ("approximate wait for a new request when QC works at
capacity").

Numbers (illustrative):
   Normal submissions 35 per week; an extra 30 per week during the
   construction peak, from week 10 to week 30; inspection capacity 50 per
   week; 10 % rejected; repair capacity 10 per week.

KPIs: Awaiting inspection (minimize), Inspection waiting time (target 1 week),
Open NCRs (minimize).
Reference mode: "Backlog outlives the peak" (historical).
Excluded: defects missed by inspection (detection assumed perfect); inspectors
rushing under backlog pressure (a possible extension); crews proceeding past
hold points at risk.

Simulation: 0 to 78 weeks, DT = 1/8 week.

The arithmetic behind the pattern (a rough check you can do by hand):
   - During the peak, about 65 new requests per week arrive, plus about 5
     re-inspections of repaired items: about 70 per week, against a capacity
     of 50. The queue grows by about 20 per week for 20 weeks: roughly 400
     items, and a wait of roughly 8 weeks.
   - After the peak, about 40 per week arrive against a capacity of 50. The
     queue shrinks by only about 10 per week, so 400 items take roughly 40
     weeks to clear.
   - That is the whole story: a backlog drains only at the SPARE capacity, not
     at the full capacity. A 20-week peak leaves a 40-week hangover.
   (These are back-of-the-envelope figures; read the exact values on screen.)

Scenarios and interventions:
   - "Add inspection capacity" (60 instead of 50). Intervention "Add
     inspectors for the peak", level 12: "A capacity parameter."
   - "Improve first-time quality" (rejection 5 % instead of 10 %): "E.g.
     welder qualification and pre-inspection by the contractor." Intervention
     level 12: "Fewer rejections means less re-inspection load (a parameter in
     this model)."
   - Intervention "Publish the live inspection backlog and waiting time to
     site", level 6: "Lets supervisors level submissions ahead of the peak;
     needs a structural change to test."

What to look for:
   - Compare the two scenarios on the backlog chart. With these numbers, extra
     capacity has a bigger effect on the hangover because it doubles the spare
     capacity after the peak; better first-time quality helps steadily and
     also reduces repair work. Check this on screen; then try both together
     with a scenario of your own.
   - Think about the level-6 option: if site supervisors could see the queue,
     they could bring inspections forward before the peak and flatten it. That
     attacks the cause (the peak itself) rather than the symptom (the queue).


36. TANK DRAINING (AN ACCURACY CHECK)
-------------------------------------

What it is:
   Not a business problem but a physics check. Water drains from a tank
   through a hole in the bottom. The outflow speed follows Torricelli's law: v
   = square root of (2 x g x height). As the water level falls, the flow slows
   down.

Why it is included:
   This problem has an exact mathematical answer, so you can see how close the
   simulation gets to the truth, and how the step size and the method affect
   accuracy. It is LoopLab's way of letting you check the engine yourself.

Numbers (illustrative):
   Starting height 1 m; tank area 0.5 square metres (about 0.8 m diameter);
   outlet 0.0005 square metres (about a 25 mm hole); discharge coefficient
   0.6; gravity 9.81 m/s2.

Simulation: 0 to 600 seconds, DT = 0.5 second.
KPI: Water height (minimize).
Reference mode: "Analytic solution" (expected): the exact curve.

What you should see:
   With these values the exact answer is about 4 cm of water left after 600
   seconds (10 minutes); the tank would be empty after about 12.5 minutes. The
   Euler line should sit very close to the exact curve.

Things to try:
   - Run the scenario "RK4 integration" (same model, Runge-Kutta 4). The line
     becomes even closer to the exact answer.
   - Halve DT and run again with Euler. The error roughly halves (Euler is
     "first order").
   - Run "Larger outlet" (outlet doubled to 0.001). The tank empties about
     twice as fast.

The lesson:
   The engine is trustworthy when used sensibly, and Model Health's DT/2 check
   uses exactly this idea to warn you when your step size is too big. (The
   project's automated tests check the engine against exact solutions to
   within one part in a million with RK4.)

37. THE EIGHT ARCHETYPES, ONE BY ONE
------------------------------------

Each archetype below has the same layout:
- THE STORY: the pattern in everyday words.
- STRUCTURE: the loops involved.
- SIGNATURE: what the key indicator does over time (LoopLab's runnable version
  of each archetype is tested to produce exactly this shape).
- EPC ILLUSTRATION: the project example shipped in the app.
- WHAT TO DO: the app's intervention guidance, with the Meadows level of each
  piece of advice in brackets.
- WARNING SIGNS: what to listen for in meetings.

Sources: Senge (1990), Kim (1992), Meadows (2008), Sterman (2000).


37.1 FIXES THAT FAIL

THE STORY: A problem appears. A quick fix makes it go away. Some time later,
the fix produces a side effect that brings the problem back, often worse,
which invites more of the same fix.

STRUCTURE: A balancing loop (the fix reduces the symptom) plus a reinforcing
loop with a delay (the fix's side effect feeds the symptom).

SIGNATURE: Better before worse. After the fix starts, the symptom drops
quickly, then climbs back past its starting level.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "Chasing late vendor data by issuing 'preliminary' drawings
for construction: the field starts sooner, but revisions arrive weeks later
and generate more rework than the time saved."

WHAT TO DO:
- Before adopting a fix, map its likely side effects and their delays; watch
  the symptom over a horizon longer than the delay. [6]
- Choose or redesign a fix with fewer side effects. [12]
- Use the fix only to buy time while working on the underlying cause; stop it
  once the cause is addressed. [5]

WARNING SIGNS: "It worked last time." "We just need to do more of it." The
same fix appears in every monthly report.


37.2 SHIFTING THE BURDEN

THE STORY: A problem can be solved two ways: a quick symptomatic solution, or
a slower fundamental one. The quick one is used because it works fast. Over
time, relying on it erodes the ability to use the fundamental one, so
dependence grows. (A special case: "Shifting the burden to the intervenor",
where an outside helper takes over and the system forgets how to help itself.)

STRUCTURE: Two balancing loops (symptomatic and fundamental, the fundamental
one delayed) and a reinforcing loop through which the symptomatic solution
weakens the fundamental one.

SIGNATURE: The fundamental capability decays, settling toward a low level.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "Outsourcing every stress-analysis peak to an external
consultant: the in-house piping stress team shrinks and loses experience, so
the next peak must be outsourced too."

WHAT TO DO:
- Use the symptomatic solution only alongside the fundamental one, with a date
  to wean off it. [5]
- Make the erosion of fundamental capability visible (track skills, root-cause
  closure rate), not only the symptom. [6]
- Strengthen the fundamental solution so it responds faster (shorter delay).
  [9]
- Build the system's own capacity to solve the problem (the intervenor helps
  the system help itself). [4]

WARNING SIGNS: "We have no choice but to outsource it again." "Nobody here
knows how to do that any more." Overtime that has become permanent.


37.3 LIMITS TO GROWTH

THE STORY: Something grows because success breeds success. Then it hits a
limit it did not see coming, and growth slows or stops however hard people
push.

STRUCTURE: A reinforcing growth engine (R) meets a balancing loop (B) that
gets stronger as a limit approaches.

SIGNATURE: S-shaped growth.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "A contractor wins ever more work on its reputation until
the pool of experienced project engineers it can hire runs dry; further bids
stretch the same people and delivery quality starts to limit new awards."

WHAT TO DO:
- Do not push harder on the growth engine; find and relieve the limiting
  condition instead. [8]
- Anticipate the limit: monitor the slowing action before growth stalls. [6]
- Raise the limit (new capacity, a new market segment). [12]

WARNING SIGNS: "We're doing everything we did before, but it isn't working any
more." More effort brings smaller results.

Caution in LoopLab: the archetype matcher can wrongly suggest Limits to Growth
for simple population-style models (births and deaths). Always check the story
before confirming.


37.4 ERODING GOALS

THE STORY: There is a gap between where we are and where we want to be.
Closing it by improving performance is slow and hard. Closing it by lowering
the goal is quick and easy. Gradually the goal drifts down.

STRUCTURE: Two balancing loops that both close the gap: corrective action
(slow) and lowering the goal (fast).

SIGNATURE: The goal drifts down and does not level off.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "Weld-repair-rate targets on a piping package are relaxed
after a bad month, then again the next quarter, until a repair rate that once
triggered a stop-work is treated as normal."

WHAT TO DO:
- Anchor goals to external benchmarks or customer requirements rather than to
  past performance. [3]
- Make any change to a goal an explicit, recorded decision with a reason. [5]
- Shorten the time corrective action takes to show results. [9]

WARNING SIGNS: "That's just how it is on this kind of job." "Let's be
realistic." Targets reset each period to last period's actual.


37.5 ESCALATION

THE STORY: Two parties each see the other as a threat. Each responds to the
other's position by trying to get ahead. Each response provokes a bigger
response.

STRUCTURE: Two balancing loops (each side responding to the other's lead) that
together form a reinforcing figure of eight.

SIGNATURE: Escalation: ever faster growth on both sides.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "Contractor claims and client back-charges: each new claim
triggers counter-claims, and both sides add commercial staff and legal support
until the dispute costs more than the original variation."

WHAT TO DO:
- Find a way for both sides to meet their real goal without being ahead (e.g.
  agree on parity). [3]
- Unilateral, visible de-escalation to reverse the reinforcing loop. [7]
- Agree rules that cap the contest (e.g. price floors, claim protocols). [5]

WARNING SIGNS: "If they do that, we'll have to respond." Each side's actions
are justified only by reference to the other's.


37.6 SUCCESS TO THE SUCCESSFUL

THE STORY: Two activities compete for the same limited resource. The one that
starts slightly ahead gets more of the resource, which makes it succeed more,
which earns it even more of the resource. The other is starved and declines.

STRUCTURE: Two reinforcing loops coupled through a single allocation.

SIGNATURE: Divergence from a near-balance: one rises, the other falls.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "The strongest project in the portfolio keeps receiving the
most experienced piping and stress engineers; the weaker project slips, is
labelled 'troubled', and loses even more senior staff."

WHAT TO DO:
- Decouple the allocation from past success (allocate by need, strategy or
  fixed proportion). [5]
- Review the goal that the allocation serves; balanced capability may matter
  more than short-term returns. [3]
- Make the long-term cost of starving the other activity visible to whoever
  allocates. [6]

WARNING SIGNS: "Put the best people where they'll make the most difference."
"That project is a lost cause."


37.7 TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS

THE STORY: Several parties each benefit from using a shared resource. Each
one, acting sensibly for itself, uses more. Together they overload the
resource, and everyone loses.

STRUCTURE: Individual growth loops (R) that share one limited resource (B).

SIGNATURE: Overshoot and collapse of the total gain.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "Several subcontractors share the site's heavy-lift cranes
and lay-down area; each books more slots to protect its own schedule until the
shared plan is gridlocked and every crew waits."

WHAT TO DO:
- Agree a governance rule for the shared resource (quotas, booking,
  allocation) that all parties follow. [5]
- Give every user timely feedback on the state of the shared resource and
  their share of its use. [6]
- Change the goal from maximising individual gain to sustaining the commons.
  [3]

WARNING SIGNS: "If we don't book it, someone else will." Everyone
over-reserves "just in case".


37.8 GROWTH AND UNDERINVESTMENT

THE STORY: Demand grows. Capacity does not keep up, because investment is
decided by looking at today's performance. As performance slips, the standard
is quietly lowered, so the need to invest looks smaller, so investment stays
low, and demand eventually goes elsewhere.

STRUCTURE: A growth loop (R) limited by capacity, with investment tied to a
performance standard that erodes (an Eroding Goals inside a Limits to Growth).

SIGNATURE: Growth, then stagnation.

EPC ILLUSTRATION: "A fabrication yard wins spool orders faster than it adds
welders and QC staff; delivery performance slips, the 'acceptable' lead time
is quietly extended, and clients start placing orders elsewhere."

WHAT TO DO:
- Hold the performance standard; derive it from customer needs, not from
  recent performance. [3]
- Invest ahead of demand, sized to the growth you intend, not to today's
  shortfall. [5]
- Shorten the capacity acquisition delay (pre-qualified vendors, modular
  capacity). [9]

WARNING SIGNS: "Let's wait until demand is proven before we invest." "Clients
understand lead times are longer now."


38. MEADOWS' TWELVE LEVERAGE POINTS, ONE BY ONE
-----------------------------------------------

Donella Meadows (1999) listed twelve places to intervene in a system. Level 12
is the weakest (easiest, least effective); level 1 is the strongest (hardest,
most transforming). LoopLab tags every intervention with one of these levels
so the depth of each option is always visible.

For each level: what it means, a project example, and a practical note.

LEVEL 12 - Constants, parameters, numbers.
   Meaning: changing a number while the structure stays the same: headcount,
   budget, deadline, target, rate.
   Example: "Add two engineers." "Move the deadline to week 64."
   Note: the most common fix and usually the weakest, because the loops that
   cause the problem are left as they were. Sometimes still the right
   short-term move; rarely the whole answer.

LEVEL 11 - The sizes of buffers and stabilising stocks, relative to their
  flows.
   Meaning: how big the cushion is: inventory, float, contingency, spare
   capacity.
   Example: holding a buffer of critical spools on site; carrying schedule
   float on the critical path.
   Note: buffers absorb shocks but cost money and can hide problems.

LEVEL 10 - The structure of material stocks and flows.
   Meaning: the physical layout: routes, plants, sequences, the way material
   moves.
   Example: changing the construction sequence; adding a second fabrication
   yard; modularisation.
   Note: powerful but slow and expensive to change once built. Best decided
   early.

LEVEL 9 - The lengths of delays, relative to the rate of system change.
   Meaning: how long it takes for causes to produce effects, or for
   information to arrive.
   Example: earlier design reviews; frame agreements that shorten procurement
   lead times; faster NCR close-out.
   Note: one of the most underused levers in projects. Shortening a delay
   changes WHEN the system learns, often at low cost. Both bundled EPC
   examples show a level-9 option matching or beating a level-12 one.

LEVEL 8 - The strength of balancing (negative) feedback loops, relative to the
  impacts they are trying to correct.
   Meaning: how strongly the system's corrections respond to problems.
   Example: a stop-work authority that actually triggers; a quality gate that
   cannot be bypassed.
   Note: weak correcting loops let problems grow before anyone acts.

LEVEL 7 - The gain around driving reinforcing (positive) feedback loops.
   Meaning: how fast a vicious or virtuous circle turns.
   Example: slowing the rework spiral by protecting quality under pressure;
   de-escalating a claims dispute.
   Note: slowing a vicious circle is often more effective than speeding up the
   corrective loop that fights it.

LEVEL 6 - The structure of information flows.
   Meaning: who has access to what information, and when.
   Example: reporting progress on verified work only; publishing the live
   inspection backlog to site; sharing crane-booking status with all
   subcontractors.
   Note: very often cheap and surprisingly powerful. A missing feedback loop
   (people not seeing the consequences of their actions) is one of the most
   common causes of bad behaviour in systems.

LEVEL 5 - The rules of the system.
   Meaning: incentives, punishments, constraints, procedures, contract terms.
   Example: allocating senior engineers by need instead of by past success; a
   claims protocol; a rule that goals can only change with a recorded reason.
   Note: rules shape what everyone else does automatically. Ask "who writes
   the rules, and what do they reward?"

LEVEL 4 - The power to add, change, evolve or self-organise system structure.
   Meaning: the system's ability to change itself.
   Example: building an in-house capability instead of permanently outsourcing
   it; empowering teams to redesign their own workflow.
   Note: organisations that can learn and reorganise outlast those that
   cannot.

LEVEL 3 - The goals of the system.
   Meaning: what the whole system is actually trying to achieve, which may
   differ from what it says.
   Example: shifting from "maximise reported progress" to "finish right first
   time"; deriving the lead-time standard from customer needs.
   Note: change the goal and every lower level reorganises around it.

LEVEL 2 - The mindset or paradigm out of which the system arises.
   Meaning: the shared, unspoken beliefs that the goals, rules and structure
   all rest on.
   Example: "Schedule pressure makes people productive." "Errors are
   individual failures." Replacing them with "pressure creates rework" or
   "errors are system signals".
   Note: hard to change, but a changed paradigm changes everything downstream.

LEVEL 1 - The power to transcend paradigms.
   Meaning: holding any paradigm lightly; recognising that no single worldview
   is the whole truth.
   Example: a leadership culture that routinely asks "what if our core
   assumption is wrong?"
   Note: rarely the topic of a project decision, but it is the attitude that
   makes systems thinking possible at all.

How to use the levels in practice:
1. For every option on your list, write its level.
2. If all options are level 12 (very common), stop and look for a 9, 6 or 5
   that acts on the same loop.
3. Test both in the model. The deeper option does not always win on the
   numbers, but when it does, it usually wins for longer.
4. In the report, explain your choice of level in one sentence.


39. READING THE CHARTS
----------------------

Time-series chart (Test).
   - Time runs left to right; each line is a variable.
   - Kept runs (scenarios) appear as separate lines, so you can compare them
     directly. Toggle each run on or off in the side panel.
   - The faint line behind is the reference mode from Frame. Compare SHAPES
     first (does it rise, peak, stall at the same point?), then timing, then
     levels.
   - Look for the moment lines separate: that is when an intervention starts
     to make a difference.

Tornado chart (Test, sensitivity).
   - One horizontal bar per uncertain constant, the widest at the top.
   - Each bar runs from the KPI value with the constant at its LOW end to the
     value with it at its HIGH end, with the base run in the middle.
   - Wide bar: the outcome is sensitive to this number, so measure it
     carefully or manage it actively.
   - Narrow bar: this number hardly matters, so a rough estimate is fine.
   - Remember: it tests one constant at a time. Two constants that matter only
     together will not show up.

Pareto line (Test and Analyze).
   - The cumulative share of the total, added up from the top of the ranking.
   - Read it like this: "the first three items account for 80 %". That is your
     vital few. Everything after the line flattens out is the trivial many.

Loop dominance chart (Test).
   - For each moment in time, which loop (or smallest group of loops) accounts
     for at least half of what drives the change in the model.
   - Read it as a story: "the growth loop was in charge until week 20, then
     the limit took over".
   - Handovers are important moments. Interventions that act before a handover
     are usually far more effective than those that act after it.

KPI comparison table (Decide).
   - One row per KPI, one column per scenario, with the base run for
     reference.
   - Read it against the KPI's goal: minimize (smaller is better), maximize
     (bigger is better), or target (closer to the target is better).

Structural leverage ranking (Analyze, report).
   - Variables ranked by how central they are to the loop structure.
   - It is a structural score, not a measured effect. A high-ranked variable
     is a good place to LOOK for an intervention; the simulation tells you
     whether acting there actually helps.


40. THE COPILOT (CLAUDE INSIDE THE APP)
---------------------------------------

What it is:
   A Claude panel in the right dock, available in every stage. It reads your
   current model through a small set of safe tools and can suggest changes. It
   needs the local version and an Anthropic API key on your computer. Without
   a key (and in the online copy), the panel simply says it is not configured;

The five modes:
   INTERVIEW   Asks you questions, one at a time, to draw out your explanation
   of the problem, then proposes a causal loop diagram.
   CRITIQUE    Checks your model for convention and logic errors. Each finding
   names the exact elements concerned.
   EXPLAIN     Writes a plain-language story for each loop.
   INTERVENE   Proposes interventions with their Meadows level. It must
   simulate each one before proposing it.
   REPORT      Drafts the decision brief.

How it stays honest:
   - Tools. It can call at most 8 read-only tools per request: model summary,
     loop list, health, simulate a scenario. The tool trace is shown, so you
     can see where every number came from.
   - Numbers. It may only quote numbers returned by those tools, and it must
     label hypotheses as hypotheses.
   - You decide. Every proposed change arrives as a "patch", shown as a
     highlighted difference on the canvas. You accept or reject each change
     separately, or all at once. Nothing changes until you accept.
   - Tagging. Accepted elements stay tagged "AI-proposed" until you mark them
     confirmed, so you always know what came from the AI.
   - Bad output changes nothing. If its answer is malformed, it retries once;
     if it fails again, you see an error and your model is untouched.
   - Untrusted text. Text inside your model and imported files is treated as
     data, never as instructions to the AI.
   - Your key. It stays in the local server's ".env" file. It is never sent to
     the browser, never logged and never saved in the project.

Good ways to use it:
   - Stuck on a blank canvas: Interview mode.
   - Before showing a model to anyone: Critique mode.
   - Before a meeting: Explain mode, to get one-paragraph loop stories.
   - When you have run out of ideas: Intervene mode, then check its simulated
     results yourself.

Status: the copilot panel, its tool loop and the accept/reject patch review
are built and tested against a simulated AI. Intervene and Report work in
those server-side tests, but their in-app flows have not been fully signed
off, and no live test with a real key has been run in this project (see
chapter 44).


41. YOUR OWN MODEL FROM SCRATCH: A 10-STEP RECIPE
-------------------------------------------------

This recipe takes one to two focused hours for a first model. It is shown with
a new example that is not bundled in the app: a team that uses overtime to
clear a backlog.

The example problem:
   "We put the team on overtime to clear the backlog. Output went up for a
   month or two, then fell below where it started, while overtime stayed
   high."

STEP 1 - Write the problem as a pattern over time (Frame).
   Choose "New model" from the file menu in the top bar. In Frame, write:
   Problem: "Output rose with overtime, then fell below normal within three
   months, while the backlog kept growing."
   Purpose: "Explain the fall in output and compare overtime limits, recovery
   time and extra staff."
   Time horizon: 0 to 52 weeks.

STEP 2 - Sketch the reference mode.
   On the sketch pad, draw "Completion rate": flat, up for 6-8 weeks, then
   down below the start. Label it "historical".

STEP 3 - Choose two or three KPIs.
   Backlog (minimize) and Completion (maximize).

STEP 4 - List what you will leave out (boundary chart).
   "Staff turnover - kept simple for a first model." "Quality of work - see
   the rework example." "Weekends and holidays - averaged out."

STEP 5 - Draw the variables (Map).
   Press A for each: Backlog, New work, Completion, Overtime, Fatigue,
   Capacity.

STEP 6 - Draw the links, with signs, and close the loops.
   Press L for each link and P to flip signs where needed:
   - New work -> Backlog (+)
   - Completion -> Backlog (-)
   - Backlog -> Overtime (+)  "Managers add overtime when the backlog is
     high."
   - Overtime -> Capacity (+)
   - Overtime -> Fatigue (+, with a delay mark: press D)  "Long weeks tire
     people gradually."
   - Fatigue -> Capacity (-)  "Tired people work slower and make mistakes."
   - Capacity -> Completion (+)
   Read the loops aloud:
   - "Work harder" (B): Backlog -> Overtime -> Capacity -> Completion -> less
     Backlog. One "-" link: balancing.
   - "Burnout" (R): Backlog -> Overtime -> Fatigue -> less Capacity -> less
     Completion -> more Backlog. Two "-" links: reinforcing.

STEP 7 - Analyze.
   The app finds both loops. The archetype matcher may suggest Fixes that Fail
   (the fix is overtime; the delayed side effect is fatigue). If the story
   fits, confirm it.

STEP 8 - Quantify (one possible set of equations; all numbers illustrative).
   Stocks:
   - Backlog [tasks] = 400 (starting value)
   - Fatigue [dmnl] = 0 (starting value; 0 = rested, 1 = exhausted)
   Flows:
   - New work [tasks/week] = New_work_rate          (into Backlog, from a
     cloud)
   - Completion [tasks/week] = MIN(Capacity, Backlog /
     Minimum_completion_time)   (out of Backlog, to a cloud)
   - Tiring [1/week] = Overtime / Time_to_tire      (into Fatigue)
   - Recovery [1/week] = Fatigue / Time_to_recover  (out of Fatigue)
   Auxiliaries:
   - Overtime [dmnl] = MIN(Maximum_overtime, MAX(0, Backlog /
     (Target_clearance_time * Normal_capacity) - 1))
        (the extra fraction of hours: 0.2 means 20 % more hours)
   - Capacity [tasks/week] = Normal_capacity * (1 + Overtime) * (1 -
     Fatigue_effect * Fatigue)
   Constants (with ranges):
   - Normal_capacity = 100 tasks/week [90, 110]
   - New_work_rate = 105 tasks/week [95, 115]
   - Maximum_overtime = 0.5 [0.2, 0.5]
   - Target_clearance_time = 4 weeks [2, 8]
   - Time_to_tire = 4 weeks [2, 8]
   - Time_to_recover = 8 weeks [4, 12]
   - Fatigue_effect = 0.6 [0.3, 0.8]
   - Minimum_completion_time = 1 week [0.5, 2]
   Add an assertion: Backlog >= 0.
   Check Model Health until it is clean (units, links matching equations).

STEP 9 - Test.
   Run the base case. Does Completion rise and then fall below 100, like your
   sketch? If not, ask what is missing, rather than tuning numbers until it
   fits.
   Then create scenarios:
   - "Cap overtime at 10 %" (Maximum_overtime = 0.1)
   - "Faster recovery" (Time_to_recover = 4, e.g. guaranteed rest days)
   - "Hire" (Normal_capacity = 110)
   Run sensitivity to see which constants matter most.

STEP 10 - Decide and report.
   Record each option with its level: the overtime cap is a rule (5); faster
   recovery shortens a delay (9); hiring is a parameter (12). Compare the KPIs
   side by side, write a one-line rationale for each, choose, and export the
   report.

The general recipe, in one line per step:
   1 problem as a pattern  2 sketch it  3 pick KPIs  4 list exclusions
   5 variables  6 signed links, closed loops  7 analyze  8 equations + health
   9 run, compare, sensitivity  10 decide by leverage, export the report


42. GOOD HABITS AND COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------------

Good habits:
1. Start from the behaviour, not the structure. Sketch the pattern first; then
   ask what structure would produce it.
2. Keep the first model small: five to eight variables and two or three loops.
   A small model you understand beats a big one you do not.
3. Write a mechanism note on every important link. It turns arguments about
   conclusions into discussions about specific links.
4. Give every constant a believable range. It costs a minute and makes the
   sensitivity analysis meaningful.
5. Name your loops. "Haste makes waste" travels further in a meeting than
   "loop 7".
6. Read the excluded list aloud to someone who knows the problem.
7. Compare shapes before numbers. A model that gets the right shape with
   slightly wrong numbers is useful; one that matches the numbers with the
   wrong shape is misleading.
8. Change one thing at a time when testing, then combine.
9. Keep the base run on the chart when comparing scenarios.
10. Save a model file at the end of every session.

Common mistakes:
1. Verbs as variables ("increase hiring"). Use quantities ("Hiring rate").
2. Signs on autopilot. Ask for every link: "if the cause goes UP, does the
   effect go up or down, all else equal?"
3. Open chains. If nothing comes back round, there is no feedback and the
   model cannot explain a recurring problem.
4. Forgetting delays. Without them, models look tidy and behave nothing like
   reality.
5. Mixing stocks and flows ("backlog per week" added to "backlog"). Model
   Health's unit check will catch most of these.
6. Too short a time horizon. If the delay is 8 weeks, a 12-week run shows
   almost nothing.
7. Tuning constants until the line fits the sketch. If you need extreme values
   to fit, the structure is probably wrong.
8. Treating the result as a forecast. Use it to compare options and test
   explanations.
9. Leaving soft variables out because they are hard to measure. Morale, trust
   and perceived progress often drive the whole problem.
10. Recommending only level-12 fixes without asking whether a delay,
    information flow or rule acts on the same loop.


43. HONEST LIMITS AND CAVEATS
-----------------------------

About modelling in general:
- A model is a simplification. It is only as good as the structure you give
  it. LoopLab checks consistency (units, links, signs), not truth.
- Results are for comparing options and testing explanations, not for
  forecasting exact dates or costs.
- All numbers in the bundled examples are illustrative, chosen to show
  realistic patterns; they are not data from real projects.
- LoopLab models aggregates (a pile of 1,000 tasks), not individual activities
  with dates. It complements your schedule; it does not replace it.

About specific features:
- Loop dominance: loops whose links pass through a smoothing or delay function
  (SMTH, DELAY) are scored as zero, so their influence can be under-reported.
- Tornado: uses the first KPI, at its final value. To study a different KPI,
  put it first in the KPI list.
- One-at-a-time sensitivity cannot reveal effects that only appear when two
  uncertain values move together.
- Archetype matcher: suggestions are hints based on loop shapes. It can be
  wrong; for example, it can suggest Limits to Growth for a simple
  births-and-deaths model. Always check the story.
- Loop search: stops at 1,000 loops by default (with a warning) in very
  tangled diagrams.
- Decide's "Add scenario" changes one constant per scenario. Scenarios with
  several changes come from the examples or the copilot.
- There are no random functions inside equations, on purpose: every run is
  reproducible.

About the online copy:
- The copilot does not work there (it needs the local server and a key).
- It runs inside a protected frame, which may restrict background calculation
  and browser storage. It has not been checked by clicking through every
  stage. If something fails there, use the local version.
- Browser storage belongs to one browser on one computer. Save model files.

About testing (what has and has not been checked by the project's automated
tests):
- Checked: 839 unit tests and 9 end-to-end browser tests pass; the build, type
  checks and code-style checks are clean. The simulation engine matches exact
  mathematical solutions to within one part in a million (RK4). Loop finding,
  sign checks, loop dominance and all eight archetype behaviours have their
  own tests.
- Not checked end to end: building a six-variable diagram from scratch in the
  browser, and save -> reload in the browser (both are tested at a lower level
  only). There is no automated check yet that the secret key can never appear
  in the web app's files (a manual check found none). The speed of the
  background workers has not been timed separately. No live copilot call with
  a real key has been made.


44. WHAT IS NOT BUILT YET
-------------------------

The project's own user guide (docs/USER_GUIDE.md) describes the full design,
including some features that are not built yet. Here is exactly what is
missing:

1. Monte Carlo analysis (Latin hypercube sampling).
   Would run hundreds or thousands of simulations with all uncertain constants
   varied together, and show 5-95 % bands and which parameters matter most.
   Not built; the one-at-a-time tornado is available instead.

2. Calibration and fit statistics.
   Would fit constants to real data from a CSV file and report R-squared, MAPE
   and the Theil statistics. Not built. You can still import real data as a
   reference mode and compare by eye.

3. XMILE import and export.
   XMILE is the standard file format of other system-dynamics tools such as
   Stella. Not built, so LoopLab cannot yet exchange models with them.
   LoopLab's own JSON model files work. The planned check against the
   community test-model suite (SDXorg) is also deferred.

4. Combined leverage ranking.
   The design combines the structural score, the sensitivity results and loop
   dominance into one ranking. Not built; the report uses the structural
   ranking.

5. Copilot Intervene and Report modes in the app.
   They work in server-side tests with a simulated AI, but the in-app flows
   are not signed off and no live test has been run.

These were cut deliberately to keep the project within budget. Everything else
in this guide is built and tested.

46. FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS
------------------------------

Q: Do I need to know mathematics?
A: No for Frame, Map and Analyze, which are pure thinking and drawing, and
   already deliver most of the insight. For Quantify, you need simple
   arithmetic (multiply, divide) and the patience to write short formulas. The
   bundled examples show the patterns to copy.

Q: Do I need to be online?
A: The local version runs entirely on your computer; only the optional copilot
   talks to the internet. The online copy needs a connection to open.

Q: Is my data sent anywhere?
A: No, unless you use the copilot, which sends your model to Anthropic's API
   to answer. The online copy runs in your browser.

Q: How long does a first model take?
A: One to two focused hours for a small model. Most of the value often arrives
   in the first 30 minutes, in Frame and Map.

Q: How big can a model be?
A: The engine handles hundreds of variables quickly (500 variables, 10,000
   steps in about a third of a second). But a model you can explain on one
   screen is usually more useful than a big one.

Q: The model does not match my reference mode. Is it broken?
A: Not necessarily. A mismatch means the structure is missing something: a
   delay, a loop, a limit. That is a finding. Ask "what would have to be true
   for the real pattern to happen?" and add it.

Q: Which method should I use, Euler or RK4?
A: Euler, unless you have a smooth physical model and want extra accuracy. If
   Model Health warns about integration error, reduce DT first.

Q: What does "dmnl" mean?
A: Dimensionless: a pure number or fraction with no unit, such as quality
   (0.8) or schedule pressure (1.2).

Q: Can I use real project data?
A: Yes, as a reference mode imported from CSV (time and value columns), to
   compare against the simulation by eye. Automatic fitting (calibration) is
   not built yet.

Q: Can I share a model with a colleague?
A: Save it as a model file (JSON) from the file menu and send the file. They
   open it from the file menu.

Q: Can I open models from Stella, Vensim or other tools?
A: Not yet. XMILE import is not built.

Q: Why are there no random functions?
A: So that every run of the same model gives exactly the same result.
   Uncertainty is explored by the sensitivity analysis instead.

Q: The copilot panel says "not configured". What do I do?
A: Nothing is wrong. It needs the local version with an Anthropic API key in
   the ".env" file. Everything else works without it.

Q: What if I make a mistake?
A: Ctrl+Z undoes up to 200 steps. Model files from older versions open
   automatically; damaged files are refused without touching your current
   model.

Q: Is LoopLab a replacement for my schedule or cost estimate?
A: No. It explains and tests the dynamics that bend schedules and budgets. Use
   it alongside them.

Q: Who should I show the report to?
A: Anyone who must approve or challenge the decision. Its first section is
   short enough for a busy reader; the appendix lets a specialist check every
   assumption.


47. GLOSSARY
------------

Archetype - A recurring loop structure with a typical story, shape and remedy
  (eight are included).
Assertion - A rule you write that must always hold (e.g. Backlog >= 0);
  checked at every saved step.
Auxiliary - A variable calculated from others at each moment; not a stock.
Balancing loop (B) - A loop that counteracts change and seeks a goal or limit.
  Odd number of "-" links.
Betweenness - How often a variable lies on the shortest paths between other
  variables.
Boundary chart - The lists of what is inside (endogenous), outside (exogenous)
  and deliberately left out (excluded).
Calibration - Fitting constants to real data (not built yet).
Causal link - An arrow meaning "a change here causes a change there".
CLD (causal loop diagram) - The qualitative map of variables and signed links.
Cloud - The edge of a stock-and-flow diagram, where flows come from or go to
  outside the model.
Constant - A fixed number, optionally with an uncertainty range.
Copilot - The optional Claude assistant in the right dock.
CSV - A simple spreadsheet file format (comma-separated values).
Delay - A time gap between cause and effect. Material delays move things;
  information delays move beliefs.
Delay mark - The marking on a link that shows a significant delay.
dmnl - Dimensionless (no unit).
DT - The simulation step size.
Endogenous - Explained by the model's own loops.
Euler - The default, simple step-by-step simulation method.
Exogenous - Coming from outside the model, not affected by it.
Feedback loop - A closed chain of causal links.
Flow - A rate that fills or drains a stock.
Hub - A variable that many loops pass through.
Inspector - The right-dock panel showing the details of what is selected.
Intervention - An option you consider, with a leverage level, a scenario, a
  status and a rationale.
KPI - Key performance indicator: the few variables you judge success by.
Leverage point - A place to intervene in a system; Meadows ranked twelve.
Lookup - A graphical function: a small table that describes a curved effect.
Loop dominance - Which loop is in charge of the model's behaviour at each
  moment.
Loops That Matter (LTM) - The published method LoopLab uses to measure loop
  dominance.
Model Health - The built-in checker for units, links, signs, loops and numeric
  problems.
Monte Carlo - Many runs with all uncertain values varied together (not built
  yet).
Mechanism note - A one-sentence explanation of why a link exists.
NCR - Non-conformance report: a recorded quality defect that must be repaired.
Oscillation - Repeated swinging above and below a target.
Pareto line - The cumulative share of a ranked list; shows the "vital few".
Patch - A set of changes proposed by the copilot, accepted or rejected one by
  one.
Polarity - The sign of a link: + (same direction), - (opposite), ? (unknown).
Reference mode - A sketch or data series of the behaviour you want to explain.
Reinforcing loop (R) - A loop that amplifies change. Even number of "-" links.
RK4 - Runge-Kutta 4, a more accurate simulation method for smooth models.
Scenario - A named set of changes to the base model, for comparison.
SFD (stock-and-flow diagram) - The quantitative view of the same model.
Simulation - Running the model forward in time, step by step.
Stock - An accumulation (backlog, inventory, staff); changes only through its
  flows.
System - Connected parts that together produce a pattern over time.
Tornado chart - A ranking of how much each uncertain constant swings a KPI.
U loop - A loop of unknown type, because it passes through a "?" link.
Unit - What a quantity is measured in (tasks, weeks, tasks/week).
Variable - Anything that can go up or down over time.
XMILE - The standard file format for system-dynamics models (import/export not
  built yet).


48. ONE-PAGE CHEAT SHEET
------------------------

THE IDEA
   Test a fix on the structure of the problem before spending real money and
   months on it.

THE SIX STAGES
   1 FRAME     problem as a pattern over time; horizon; 2-4 KPIs; sketch;
   exclusions
   2 MAP       variables (nouns) + signed links; close the loops; note delays
   3 ANALYZE   read the loops (R/B), hubs, archetype hints; confirm or reject
   4 QUANTIFY  stocks, flows, equations, units, ranges; keep Model Health
   clean
   5 TEST      run; scenarios side by side; tornado; loop dominance
   6 DECIDE    interventions with Meadows levels; compare KPIs; export report

KEYS (Map)
   A add   L link   P flip sign   D delay   Del delete   Shift+L layout
   F fit   Ctrl+Z undo   Ctrl+Y redo   ? shortcut sheet

LOOP RULE
   Count the "-" links: even = Reinforcing (snowball), odd = Balancing
   (thermostat).

STOCK OR FLOW?
   Countable in a photograph = stock. Needs a stopwatch = flow.
   Stock units = flow units x time.

SHAPES -> STRUCTURES
   exponential = R in charge        goal seeking = B in charge
   S-curve = R hands over to B      oscillation = B with a delay
   better-before-worse = Fixes that Fail
   last 10 % takes forever = hidden rework found late

LEVERAGE (12 weakest -> 1 strongest)
   12 numbers  11 buffers  10 physical structure  9 delays
   8 balancing strength  7 reinforcing gain  6 information
   5 rules  4 self-organisation  3 goals  2 paradigm  1 transcending
   If every option is a 12, look for a 9, 6 or 5 on the same loop.

THE EPC LESSON IN ONE LINE
   Earlier reviews (a delay, level 9) nearly match two extra engineers (level
   12); a later deadline alone barely helps; honest progress reporting (level
   6) fixes the blind spot.

BEFORE YOU TRUST A RESULT
   Model Health clean?  Shape matches the reference mode?  Ranges sensible?
   Excluded list read by someone else?  Compared against the base run?
